# The Proposal Field Guide

A practical reference for capture, writing, review, and submission on federal proposals for small business contractors.

## About this guide

This guide is for the people doing the work. The owner-operator who writes their own proposals between client deliverables. The capture manager wearing three hats. The proposal manager who joined a small business after a stint at a large prime and now has half the resources and twice the responsibility.

It assumes federal procurement context. Civilian agencies, defense, intelligence community, full and open or set-aside. The principles apply across the board. Specific examples lean toward IT services, professional services, and construction-adjacent work, since that is where most small business GovCon activity sits.

It does not assume you have a proposal shop, a graphics team, or a senior reviewer on retainer. It assumes you have one to five people, a deadline, and an obligation to submit something compliant and competitive.

The guide is organized as ten chapters and a working appendix. Read it through once. Then come back to specific chapters when you need them.

# 1. The proposal lifecycle

The most common way small businesses lose proposals is to skip straight from RFP to drafting.

The RFP arrives. Someone opens a Word document. They start writing the technical approach. Three days in, they realize the page limit applies to a different volume than they assumed. Two days before submission, they discover the SOW has a requirement nobody assigned. The proposal goes out compliant on paper and uncompetitive in substance.

Federal proposal work is a lifecycle, not a writing assignment. Five phases:

1. **Capture.** Intelligence-gathering and positioning that happens before the RFP drops. Done well, it tells you whether to bid, what to bid, and what story to tell.
2. **Pre-proposal setup.** The shred, the proposal plan, the kickoff. The structured handoff from capture to writing.
3. **Development.** Section drafting, graphics, win theme integration, internal coordination.
4. **Review.** Color team passes (Pink, Red, Gold) and editing for one voice.
5. **Production and submission.** Formatting, QC, packaging, delivery, debrief.

Each phase has decisions and deliverables. Skip a phase and the next one absorbs the work, badly.

The smaller your team, the more discipline this lifecycle requires. A large prime can afford to be sloppy in capture and recover in development, because they have the bench to throw bodies at the problem. A three-person small business cannot. Your only protection against late-stage chaos is early-stage rigor.

[FIGURE 1: Proposal lifecycle. Five phases shown left to right with decision gates between each (Bid/No-Bid → Kickoff → Pink → Red → Submit). Caption: "Each gate is a chance to fix course. Miss the gate and the cost compounds."]

A working schedule, working backward from the due date, looks like this for a typical 30-day RFP:

| Day | Phase | Key deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| RFP -30 | Capture review | Bid/no-bid decision |
| RFP -28 | Pre-proposal | Shred complete, kickoff held |
| RFP -20 | Development | 50% draft |
| RFP -14 | Review | Pink team |
| RFP -7 | Review | Red team |
| RFP -3 | Production | Gold team, white glove |
| RFP -1 | Submission | Submit (do not wait until day-of) |

If your RFP cycle is shorter, every phase compresses but none disappear. A 10-day RFP still needs capture, shred, draft, review, and production. It just gets brutal.

# 2. Roles and team sizing

Every federal proposal has the same set of jobs. The number of people doing them changes with team size and deal complexity.

**The roles:**

- **Capture/BD lead.** Owns pre-RFP intelligence, opportunity qualification, teaming decisions, and win strategy. Makes the bid/no-bid recommendation.
- **Proposal manager.** Owns the process. Schedule, compliance matrix, kickoff, color team logistics, submission. Acts as the single point of contact for status.
- **Technical lead or PM.** Owns the solution. The approach, methodology, staffing, schedule, and price basis.
- **Writers.** Subject matter experts who draft assigned sections. May be internal staff or teaming partners.
- **Reviewers.** Color team members who pressure-test the draft against compliance, evaluation criteria, and competitive position.
- **Editor.** Edits the full document for one voice, plain language, and consistency. Comes in late and reads with fresh eyes.
- **Production lead.** Owns formatting, assembly, packaging, and delivery. Often combined with proposal manager on small teams.

**Team sizing:**

A strategic must-win pursuit might run with a full team: dedicated capture lead, separate proposal manager, technical PM, multiple writers, three or more reviewers, dedicated editor, dedicated production. This is what large primes do.

A local must-win pursuit at a small business typically combines roles. One person runs capture and proposal management. The technical PM is also the lead writer for technical sections. Reviewers double as occasional writers. The editor is whoever has the strongest editing skills, brought in for the last 72 hours.

A quick-turn or on-call submittal might run with one proposal manager doing everything except technical content, plus one technical reviewer.

The role that should not be combined with anything else is the editor. Editing for one voice requires fresh eyes. If your proposal manager is also your editor, your editor read the document fifteen times before the final pass and cannot see what is on the page anymore. Borrow editing from outside the proposal team if you have to.

**The capture-to-proposal handoff.**

This is where small business proposals frequently fall apart. The capture lead has a head full of intelligence about the customer, the competition, and the strategy. The proposal manager has the RFP. If the handoff is verbal, half the intelligence evaporates.

A capture plan, written down, is the bridge. It does not have to be long. It has to exist. A two-page capture plan that names the agency hot buttons, the incumbent (if any), the likely evaluation panel, the recommended set-aside, and the win strategy is worth more than a fifty-page document nobody updates.

# 3. Pre-RFP intelligence and capture

If you start capture when the RFP drops, you have lost.

By the time the solicitation hits SAM.gov, the agency has already had its requirements shaped. Probably by the incumbent, possibly by another vendor with strong customer relationships. The selection panel has already heard about the work. The vehicle has already been chosen. The set-aside has already been decided.

Your job in capture is to be one of the vendors that shaped the procurement, or at minimum, the vendor that understood it before it dropped.

**What you need to know:**

- The agency mission and current pain points. Read their strategic plan, IG reports, OIG findings, congressional testimony. Their priorities are public.
- The incumbent, if any. Performance, contract history, expiring date, any CPARS issues, any leadership turnover.
- Your existing relationship with the agency. Past contracts, current contracts, RFI responses you submitted, conferences you attended.
- The likely set-aside category. WOSB, HUBZone, 8(a), SDVOSB, or full and open. This drives positioning and teaming.
- The likely contract vehicle. GSA MAS, OASIS+, GWAC, BPA, or open market. This drives pricing strategy and competitive set.
- The funding signals. FY budget, OMB priorities, congressional appropriations.
- The evaluation panel. Who will read the proposal. Their backgrounds. What they care about.

You will not get all of this. You will get some of it, and the gaps tell you where to push during the RFI cycle, industry days, and one-on-one meetings.

**The capture plan.**

A capture plan is a working document, not a deliverable to a customer. Keep it tight. The version that actually gets used in proposal development includes:

- Opportunity summary (what they are buying, scope, value, timing)
- Funding and timing analysis
- Competitive analysis (likely bidders, your strengths and weaknesses against each)
- Proposed teaming approach
- Set-aside positioning recommendation
- Vehicle recommendation
- Win themes (3 to 5 strategic reasons you win)
- Hot buttons identified for the evaluation panel

When the RFP drops, the capture plan gets compared against actual requirements. Mismatches are flags. If you assumed full and open and it came out 8(a) sole-source, your capture missed something. Find out what.

**RFI responses are capture, not proposal.**

When an agency releases an RFI or sources sought notice, the response is not a small proposal. It is a capture deliverable.

Three goals for an RFI response:

1. Influence the requirement. Recommend scope, standards, set-aside, vehicle.
2. Position your company. Two or three relevant past performances, not your full portfolio.
3. Build agency familiarity, especially for new relationships.

Do not include pricing. Do not include compliance matrices. Do not write a thirty-page response when the RFI asks for ten. The RFI response is the agency's first impression of your company, and a generic capability dump signals that you do not understand the customer.

# 4. Shred discipline: compliance matrix and annotated outline

A shred is the structured response framework you build from the solicitation before any writing starts.

It is not a draft. It is the scaffolding. Done right, it makes non-compliance impossible and gives every writer exactly what to address.

Three artifacts every shred produces:

1. **Acquisition overview.** Plain-language summary of what the agency is buying, how, and on what timeline.
2. **Compliance matrix.** Every requirement from Sections L and M (and any SOW or SOO references) mapped to a proposal section.
3. **Annotated outline.** One per volume. Each section contains the verbatim instructions, evaluation criteria, and SOW text relevant to that section, plus a placeholder for the response.

**The compliance matrix.**

A compliance matrix is a table. Standard columns:

| Column | What it captures |
|---|---|
| ITO Reference | Section L paragraph or page |
| Evaluation Reference | Section M criterion |
| SOW Reference | SOW or SOO paragraph |
| Proposal Section # | Hierarchical number you assigned |
| Proposal Heading | Full section title |
| Page Limit | Section-level page cap |
| Author | Person responsible |
| Status | Draft, Pink, Red, Final |

Build the matrix from Section L (instructions) and Section M (evaluation criteria) together. A common trap: a Section M criterion with no matching Section L instruction. Evaluators will still score you against it. Add a row, flag it, and assign a writer to address it somewhere logical.

Mandatory language matters. Mark every "shall" and "must" as mandatory. Mark "should" and "may" as discretionary. Mandatory items missed are non-compliant. Discretionary items missed lower your score but do not kill the bid.

**The annotated outline.**

For each volume, the annotated outline is a Word document with:

- A cover letter template
- A table of contents matching the compliance matrix
- One section block per row in the matrix

Each section block contains:

- The section heading with page limit and evaluation reference visible
- A small instruction table with verbatim ITO text, verbatim evaluation text, and verbatim SOW text
- A placeholder note ("Insert response below. Delete this table when finalized.")

Verbatim matters. Do not paraphrase the requirement. Writers must see what the government actually said, not your interpretation of it.

**Compliance vs. responsiveness.**

These are two different tests.

- **Compliant** means you included a response to every required item. The government can find an answer to every requirement.
- **Responsive** means your response actually addresses what they asked. Your answer makes sense and earns the points.

A compliant proposal can lose. A non-compliant proposal often does not get scored. You need both.

A practical example. The RFP says: "The Offeror shall describe its approach to data migration, including risk mitigation."

A compliant-but-unresponsive answer: "Our approach to data migration includes risk mitigation."

A compliant-and-responsive answer: "Our migration approach uses a four-stage process: discovery, mapping, validation, cutover. Risk mitigation includes parallel operation for two weeks, rollback procedures verified at every checkpoint, and a designated migration lead with twelve years of federal data migration experience. On the BFS FORMS migration, this approach moved 191 million data objects with zero data loss."

The first answer ticks the compliance box. The second one earns the points.

# 5. Win themes and customer-first writing

A win theme is a strategic reason you win. Most proposals need three to five.

Each theme is built from four pieces:

| Element | What it is |
|---|---|
| Feature | What you offer (a capability, approach, tool, credential) |
| Benefit | What it means for the customer (outcome, risk reduction, cost savings) |
| Discriminator | Why competitors cannot match it |
| Proof point | Specific, quantifiable evidence |

Without all four, you do not have a theme. You have a boast, a feature list, or a marketing tagline.

A worked example for a small business with a direct-delivery model:

- **Feature:** Owner-operator delivery. The principal leads every engagement.
- **Benefit:** No handoff risk. Consistent quality. Single accountability.
- **Discriminator:** Competitors use junior staff after award and reserve senior staff for the next pursuit.
- **Proof:** Three named past performances where the same principal led the work end to end. Client testimonials confirming this.

Once the themes are set, they appear throughout the proposal. Not just in the executive summary. Every section reinforces them with a proof point.

**Stated needs vs. real needs.**

The RFP tells you the stated needs. The capture plan tells you the real needs.

| Stated need | Real need |
|---|---|
| Modernize the case management system | Reduce attorney admin time and prove ROI to OMB |
| Implement a records management platform | Survive the next NARA audit |
| Provide accessibility compliance services | Avoid an OCR complaint and the political fallout |

A proposal that addresses only stated needs reads like a vendor pitch. A proposal that addresses real needs reads like a partner who understands the agency.

**The four questions.**

Apply these to every claim in the proposal:

1. **Why us?** What in our team, approach, or experience uniquely qualifies us?
2. **Why not them?** Where does the competition fall short, without naming them?
3. **So what?** What does this claim mean for the agency? What is the benefit?
4. **How so?** Where is the proof?

The fourth one is the test most proposals fail. Claims without proof read as marketing. Claims with proof read as evidence.

**Customer-first voice.**

Read your draft and count two things in the first paragraph of each section:

- How many times you used "we" or your company name
- How many times you used "you" or the agency name

If "we" outnumbers "you" by more than two-to-one, the section is seller-focused. Rewrite. Lead with the agency's mission, problem, or outcome. Then bring in your capability.

A bad opening: "ABC Company has over 20 years of experience in federal IT services."

A better opening: "Your modernization timeline is driven by three OMB deadlines in FY26. ABC's case management work for DOJ Civil Division was delivered fifteen percent ahead of schedule across every task order."

The second version puts the agency first, makes the experience relevant, and quantifies the proof.

**What to cut.**

These words should not appear in your proposal unless followed immediately by specific evidence:

- world-class
- cutting-edge
- best-in-class
- robust
- seamless
- holistic
- transformative
- proven track record
- synergy
- leverage
- best practices

When an evaluator sees "world-class" without quantification, they read it as a vendor signal. When they see "delivered 191 million data objects with zero data loss," they read it as proof.

# 6. Graphics that earn their place

A good proposal graphic communicates within eight seconds.

That is the rule. Hold a graphic up. Have a colleague look at it. If they cannot tell you the point in eight seconds, the graphic has failed.

**Action captions.**

The caption does as much work as the graphic. Standard formula: Feature + Verb + Benefit.

| Bad caption | Better caption |
|---|---|
| Figure 1: Org Chart | Figure 1: Direct line from PM to government PM eliminates handoff delays |
| Figure 2: Project Schedule | Figure 2: Phased delivery accelerates first capability to Day 90 |
| Figure 3: Risk Matrix | Figure 3: Three top risks identified and mitigated through proven approach |

The caption tells the evaluator what to take away. If they only read captions (and many do), they should still get the message.

**When graphics work:**

- Process or sequence (workflow, timeline, phased approach)
- Comparison (your approach vs. an alternative, before vs. after)
- Relationship (organization chart, system integration, data flow)
- Data (metrics, ratings, performance, growth)

**When graphics fail:**

- Decoration. A photo of a server room next to your cybersecurity section adds nothing.
- Restated bullets in shape form. If your "graphic" is three rectangles labeled with the same words as the bullet list above it, delete the rectangles.
- Clip art. Always.
- Concept graphics with no specific content. Generic "innovation" or "synergy" diagrams read as filler.

**Concept graphics first.**

Build the graphics list before writing the section. Sketch each graphic with its caption. The graphic anchors the structure. Many sections are easier to write once you know the visual that supports them.

This is also when you catch graphics that should not exist. If you cannot draft a Feature + Verb + Benefit caption for a graphic, the graphic does not have a point.

# 7. Color team reviews

A color team is a structured review pass against a specific test. Each color catches different things.

| Review | Draft state | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Pink Team | 30 to 50% draft | Strategy and structure check before writing locks in |
| Red Team | 80 to 90% draft | Full compliance and quality review, evaluator simulation |
| Gold Team | Final draft | Production-ready check, executive sign-off |
| White Glove | Post-Gold | Final proofread, formatting verification, compliance matrix re-check |

**Pink Team.**

Pink Team is early. The draft is rough. The graphics are placeholders. Reviewers are checking strategy, not prose.

What Pink should catch:

- Are the win themes appearing in every section?
- Does the structure follow the compliance matrix?
- Are there any fatal flaws (missed requirements, scope mismatches, teaming issues)?
- Is the technical approach the right approach?

Pink is the cheapest place to fix structural problems. After Pink, the cost of major rework triples.

**Red Team.**

Red Team is late. The draft is 90% complete with developed graphics, full content, and a near-final price. Reviewers simulate the evaluator.

What Red should catch:

- Compliance gaps (every shall and must accounted for)
- Responsiveness gaps (do answers actually answer)
- Win themes substantiated with proof points
- Customer-first voice
- Graphic captions following Feature + Verb + Benefit
- Math errors, version inconsistencies, naming inconsistencies

Red Team feedback should be specific. "Section 3.2 reads as feature-focused. Recommend rewriting paragraph 1 to lead with the customer outcome and move the company description to paragraph 2." That is useful. "Not clear" is not.

**Gold Team.**

Gold Team is the final go/no-go. Executive review. Approval to submit.

**White Glove.**

White Glove is a checklist pass. Page numbers right. Headers correct. TOC matches. Forms signed. Compliance matrix updated. Submission package complete.

**Editing for one voice.**

A proposal with five writers reads like five different documents unless someone edits for one voice. This is a separate pass from any color team review.

The editor is looking for:

- Tense consistency (present, future, or past, applied consistently)
- Active vs. passive voice (active by default)
- Acronym discipline (defined on first use, used consistently after)
- Term consistency (the customer is "the agency" or "DOJ" or "the Government," pick one)
- Sentence rhythm (short, medium, long, varied within paragraphs)

This pass works best with a fresh reader. Borrow one if you have to.

> **A note on AI-assisted editing.** If you are using AI to clean up writing, see Chapter 8 for guardrails. Most AI editing makes proposal writing more generic, not less.

# 8. AI in proposal writing: keeping a human voice

AI writing tools are now standard equipment in proposal shops. Used well, they speed up first drafts, compliance extraction, grammar passes, and boilerplate edits. Used poorly, they make every proposal sound like every other proposal, and federal evaluators have started noticing.

This chapter is about using AI without leaving fingerprints.

## Where AI helps

- **Compliance matrix initial extraction.** AI can read Section L and produce a first-pass requirement list. You verify and correct.
- **Past performance narrative drafts.** You provide the bullets, the metrics, and the relevance map. AI structures the narrative. You rewrite for voice.
- **Boilerplate edits.** AI can adapt your approved boilerplate to a new opportunity. Faster than starting blank, less risky than copy-paste.
- **Grammar and consistency passes.** AI catches what tired editors miss.
- **Outline brainstorming.** AI generates structure options you can pick from.

## Where AI hurts

- **Win theme development.** AI generates generic themes. Your themes need agency-specific intelligence.
- **Anything requiring capture intel.** AI does not know your customer.
- **Resumes.** AI inflates. Federal proposals require restraint.
- **Anything that needs to sound like a specific person.** AI defaults to a corporate-neutral voice.
- **First-person company language.** AI does not understand your differentiators. It generates competitor-interchangeable claims.

## The AI tells evaluators notice

Three patterns identify AI text reliably. If your proposal contains any of them, evaluators with experience reading AI output will register the document as machine-generated, even if the underlying content is good.

### Vocabulary fingerprint

Specific words appear in AI text at much higher rates than in human writing. They are statistically overrepresented in LLM output. The most relevant for federal proposals:

| Banned word | Why it is a tell |
|---|---|
| leverage | Default AI verb when "use" would do |
| robust | Filler adjective with no content |
| seamless | Almost never true, almost always AI |
| holistic | Vague, AI-favored |
| transformative | Inflates ordinary work |
| optimize | Generic, content-free |
| foster | AI's go-to for "support" |
| accentuate | AI synonym substitution |
| garner | AI synonym substitution |
| harness | AI verb for "use" |
| unlock | Hype language |
| delve | Universally AI |
| intricate / intricacies | AI dramatizes |
| meticulously | AI inflates effort |
| synergy | Banned in business writing for two decades, AI still uses it |
| paradigm | Abstract, AI-favored |
| pivotal | AI inflates significance |
| streamline | Often empty |
| empower | Vague |
| innovative | AI's default modifier |
| cutting-edge | Hype |
| state-of-the-art | Hype |
| game-changer | Hype |
| revolutionize | Hype |

Find-and-replace pass before any AI-drafted section ships. Most of these can be deleted with no loss of meaning. Some need a real verb in their place. "Leverage" almost always becomes "use." "Robust" almost always deletes cleanly. "Foster" usually becomes "support" or "build."

### Negative parallelisms

This is the single most reliable AI tell. The pattern negates a framing, then asserts a corrected one.

Examples:

- "It's not just about the technology, it's about the outcome."
- "This isn't another modernization. This is a transformation."
- "Forget legacy thinking. Embrace the future."
- "The question isn't whether to migrate. The question is when."
- "You don't need a vendor. You need a partner."
- "Stop thinking in projects. Start thinking in programs."

Variations the AI uses to disguise the same pattern:

- "While X might seem important, Y is actually..."
- "Sure, X works, but Y is where the real..."
- "X gets all the attention, but Y is what actually delivers..."

The fix: delete the negation. Just say what you mean. "It is about the outcome." "This is a transformation." "Embrace the future." Most of the time, even the positive half is fluff and can also go.

When you see this pattern in an AI draft, rewrite the sentence. Do not edit it. Rewrite it.

### Other patterns

A short field guide:

- **Rule of three.** AI loves listing three adjectives or phrases. "Speed, efficiency, and innovation." Three short clauses. Use two, or four, or just say the one thing that matters.
- **False ranges.** "From legacy systems to modern platforms." "From small projects to enterprise modernization." If you cannot identify meaningful middle ground, the range is fake.
- **Inflated significance.** "A pivotal moment." "A key inflection point." "A transformative shift." Cut. State the fact. Let the reader judge significance.
- **Participle phrases that pretend to add depth.** "Highlighting our commitment to excellence." "Underscoring our experience." "Reflecting broader trends." Delete. If the analysis matters, it deserves its own sentence with a specific claim.
- **Title case in headers.** AI capitalizes every word: "Global Context: Critical Mineral Demand." Federal proposals use sentence case. Match the convention.
- **Copulative avoidance.** AI replaces "is" and "has" with "serves as," "stands as," "represents," "marks." Just say "is."
- **Meta commentary.** "In this section, we will describe..." Cut. Say the thing.

## Compliance and security

Brief but important.

- Do not paste sensitive solicitation text into a public LLM. Some RFPs are FOUO or CUI. Some agencies prohibit it explicitly.
- Do not paste customer-confidential capture intelligence.
- Confirm your AI tool is acceptable for the work. FedRAMP-authorized tools are safer for federal work. Air-gapped or on-premises deployments are required for some agencies.
- Read the terms of service. Some free tools train on your input.

If you are not sure whether a tool is approved for a given proposal, ask your contracting officer or your information security team. Default to no.

## The AI editing pass

When you draft any section with AI assistance, run a deliberate edit pass before it joins the proposal:

1. **Banned word sweep.** Find-and-replace the worst offenders.
2. **Negative parallelism check.** Read every paragraph. Any "X. Y." or "not just X, Y" patterns get rewritten.
3. **Read aloud.** Anything that sounds like a TED talk or a corporate keynote, rewrite. Federal evaluators do not want to be inspired. They want to be informed.
4. **Specifics check.** Add numbers, names, dates, agencies. AI defaults to abstraction. Replace abstractions with proof.
5. **Stance check.** What does your company actually think? AI hedges. Take a position.
6. **Customer-first check.** Does the section lead with the agency, or with you?

This pass takes thirty to sixty minutes per section. It is the difference between an AI-assisted proposal that reads as your work and an AI-assisted proposal that reads as machine output.

## A practical example

Here is an AI-drafted opening paragraph for a hypothetical management approach section:

> "ABC Company is committed to delivering robust, transformative solutions that leverage cutting-edge methodologies to optimize agency outcomes. Our holistic approach fosters seamless collaboration, harnessing innovative best practices to empower stakeholders and unlock unprecedented value. It's not just about delivering services. It's about transforming the way the agency operates."

Word count: 56. Meaning: zero.

Here is the human edit:

> "Your modernization is on a 24-month clock with three OMB checkpoints. ABC Company will run the program as we ran the FAA case management modernization in 2023: phased delivery, fixed-price structure, and the same project manager from kickoff through closeout. The first capability ships in 90 days."

Word count: 53. Meaning: a specific approach, a specific past performance, a specific commitment, and a specific milestone.

Same length. Different document.

# 9. Production, QC, and submission

The final week is when good proposals fall apart for boring reasons.

Page count creeps over the limit. A graphic gets resized and breaks. The compliance matrix shows a missing form that nobody noticed. The submission portal returns an error two hours before the deadline.

This chapter is about preventing those failures.

## The final 72 hours

A typical end-of-cycle schedule:

| Day | Activity |
|---|---|
| Day -3 | Gold Team review and executive approval |
| Day -2 | Final edits incorporated, formatting locked |
| Day -1 | White Glove pass, production, packaging, electronic copy verified |
| Day 0 | Submission, confirmation receipt, internal notification |

Submit before the deadline. Not at the deadline. Federal submission portals fail. Email gets quarantined. Hand delivery hits traffic.

The cost of submitting six hours early is zero. The cost of submitting six minutes late is the entire proposal.

## Copyediting and proofreading are different jobs

Copyediting is content work. Clarity, consistency, voice, sentence structure, paragraph flow. Heavy edits to weak passages. This happens during and after color teams.

Proofreading is mechanical work. Spelling, punctuation, formatting, page numbers, table of contents accuracy, header consistency, acronym usage. This happens after the content is locked.

A common error: trying to proofread while still copyediting. The mind cannot do both. Separate the passes. Lock content first, then proofread.

The proofreader should not be someone who has been writing the proposal. Fresh eyes catch what tired eyes miss.

## The final page turn

A simple ritual that catches assembly errors:

1. Print or pull up one master copy and as many duplicates as you have submission requirements.
2. Assign each copy to a person.
3. The master holder reads aloud: "Volume One, Cover Letter, page i. Table of Contents, pages ii through iv. Executive Summary, pages 1 through 3."
4. Every other person flips through their copy and confirms the same content is in the same place.

This catches missing pages, duplicated sections, swapped volumes, wrong page numbers, missing forms, and broken page breaks.

It takes twenty minutes for a typical proposal. It is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

## Submission methods

Federal submissions come through several channels:

- **SAM.gov.** Many federal acquisitions. Has its own quirks. Test the upload mechanism well before deadline.
- **Agency portals.** Many agencies use their own systems (Acquisition.gov, eBuy, agency-specific).
- **Email.** Common for small contracts and RFIs. Confirm file size limits and delivery confirmation.
- **Hand delivery.** Rare but happens. Bring a backup electronic copy on a thumb drive.

For every submission, capture:

- The submission timestamp
- The confirmation number or receipt
- Screenshots of the upload confirmation page
- A copy of the confirmation email

If a dispute arises later, this documentation is the difference between a confirmed submission and a missed deadline.

## Backup plans

Always have one.

- If the portal fails, what is the alternative submission method?
- If your internet goes down on submission day, where is the backup connection?
- If the file gets corrupted, where is the backup file?
- If the submitter is unavailable, who is the backup submitter?

Rare events happen on submission day specifically because submission day is the day they would matter most.

# 10. Debrief and lessons learned

After submission, two debriefs.

## Internal debrief

Within 48 hours of submission, while the experience is fresh.

Five questions:

1. What slipped against our schedule, and why?
2. Where did we recover? What worked?
3. What would we do differently next time?
4. What approved boilerplate, graphics, or past performance came out of this proposal that we should add to our reusable library?
5. Who deserves recognition?

Write the answers down. File them where the next proposal team will find them. Most lessons learned die because nobody reads them.

## Client debrief

Always request, on a win or a loss.

On a loss, the debrief tells you what to fix. On a win, it tells you why you won, which is harder to know than it seems and more useful to know than it seems.

Some agencies do written debriefs only. Some do oral. Some require formal requests within a window. Read the agency's debrief procedures and follow them.

Questions worth asking, organized by what you want to learn:

**Score and ranking:**

- What was our overall score?
- How did we rank among offerors?
- Was the winner closer to the top of the technical range, the bottom of the price range, or both?

**Technical evaluation:**

- Where did we score strongest?
- Where did we score weakest?
- Were there any compliance findings?
- Were there any specific weaknesses called out?

**Price evaluation:**

- Was price a major factor?
- Did we score high on technical and lose on price?
- Was the winner's price significantly different from ours?

**Past performance:**

- Did our past performance score reflect strong relevance?
- Were any of our references contacted?

**Presentation:**

- Was the proposal easy to evaluate?
- Were there any structural issues that made evaluation harder?

Listen. Do not argue. Do not protest the evaluator's methodology in real time. The goal is information, not vindication.

After the debrief, write down what you heard. Update your capture playbook. Adjust your boilerplate. Refine your discriminators. The next proposal benefits from the last one only if you let it.

## F. Brief glossary

- **BPA** — Blanket Purchase Agreement. A simplified acquisition vehicle for recurring needs.
- **CLIN** — Contract Line Item Number. A pricing structure element.
- **CPARS** — Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System. Federal performance ratings.
- **FFP** — Firm Fixed Price. Contract type with set price regardless of effort.
- **GWAC** — Government-Wide Acquisition Contract. A multi-agency vehicle.
- **IDIQ** — Indefinite Delivery, Indefinite Quantity. A vehicle with task orders.
- **NAICS** — North American Industry Classification System. The code that determines size standards.
- **OASIS+** — A GSA professional services GWAC.
- **RFI** — Request for Information. Market research, not a procurement.
- **RFP** — Request for Proposal. The formal solicitation.
- **RFQ** — Request for Quotation. Often used for simpler buys.
- **SAM** — System for Award Management. The federal vendor registration and acquisition portal.
- **SOO** — Statement of Objectives. Outcomes-focused work statement.
- **SOW** — Statement of Work. Task-focused work statement.
- **T&M** — Time and Materials. Contract type billed by hours and costs.
- **WOSB / EDWOSB** — Women-Owned / Economically Disadvantaged Women-Owned Small Business set-asides.
